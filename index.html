Light Is a Carrier; a Photodiode Sees Only Power

Light at 1550 nm is a carrier at about 193 THz. Its field E(t) = A·cos(2πft + φ) has an amplitude A, which can be negative, and a phase φ, exactly like an RF carrier. No electronics can follow 193 THz: a photodiode's current is proportional to the power |E|², averaged over many cycles. textbook

Top: a field whose amplitude flips from +A to −A halfway (the carrier drawn absurdly slowly, a few cycles where light has billions). Bottom: what a photodiode reports, the power averaged over the carrier cycles, A², the same on both halves. The sign, and with it any phase, is gone.

The Mach–Zehnder Modulator

Phase is easy to change in silicon; amplitude is not. A voltage across a pn junction in the waveguide moves free carriers and shifts the refractive index (plasma dispersion: tens of GHz, but weak); a heater does the same through the thermo-optic effect (strong, but microseconds). A phase change alone is invisible to a photodiode. An interferometer makes it visible: split the light into two arms, shift their phases, and recombine, so the fields add. textbook

Δφ = φbias + π·V/Vπ    Eout = Ein·cos(Δφ/2)    Pout = Pin·cos²(Δφ/2)

Each arm carries half the input field (a phasor of length ½). Push-pull drive turns the arms equally and oppositely (+Δφ/2, −Δφ/2), so their sum (accent) always lies on the real axis: amplitude without phase wobble (no chirp), passing through zero and changing sign. Tick the box to drive one arm only: the sum then swings off the axis. Model: mzmArms.

  • Vπ is the drive that moves Δφ by π, from full power to none. Silicon depletion modulators have Vπ·L of a few V·cm (one design: 3.1 V·cm at 2 V reverse bias, Félix Rosa et al., Adv. Radio Sci. 15, 2017), so a mm-scale modulator needs several volts: hence a driver amplifier. textbook
  • The module: four MZMs from the foundry's process design kit, fed by linear “Direct-Drive MZM Drivers”, with thermo-optic phase shifters for phase control. paper

The Transfer Curve and Three Bias Points

The power transfer curve is a raised cosine. As with a transistor, you choose a DC operating point (the bias, set by a slow phase shifter inside the arms) and the data swings either side of it. Three bias points matter. textbook

Peak (Δφ = 0)

  • Full power; the curve is flat, so the first-order response is zero.
  • Rarely used for data.

Quadrature (π/2)

  • Power responds linearly and most steeply; both field levels are positive.
  • Ordinary NRZ and PAM4 datacom optics (intensity modulation, direct detection). RF analogue: AM with a carrier.

Null (π)

  • The field passes through zero linearly and changes sign; '0' and '1' have equal power.
  • Coherent formats (BPSK, QPSK, QAM). RF analogue: double-sideband suppressed carrier.

At ±0.22 Vπ the field step is 0.677 at null against 0.479 at quadrature (of the maximum field): the field is steepest at null, the power at quadrature.

“Quadrature” Has Two Meanings

Warning

In optics, “quadrature” names two different things. Ask which one is meant.

Left: a modulator biased at quadrature, the half-power point, its two arms 90° apart (slide 03). Right: I/Q quadrature, two carrier components 90° apart in phase, as in an IQ modulator (slide 05) or a 90° optical hybrid (slide 08).

  • The module's calibration patent means the first: its first calibration step is “setting a quadrature of the modulators (transmitters)”, so that “the quadrature point of all the modulators in the input channels can be matched” (WO2025168828A1, a patent cited by the paper). patent
  • The module's receivers use the second idea in a simpler way: each detector reads one projection, chosen by its LO phase (slide 08), rather than both I and Q at once.

The Textbook Coherent Transmitter

Every coherent long-haul transceiver writes its data with an IQ modulator: two MZMs, each biased at null so it is a signed, carrier-free modulator, combined 90° apart so the output is one complex field E = I + jQ. textbook (Seimetz, High-Order Modulation for Optical Fiber Transmission, 2009; Kikuchi, J. Lightwave Technol. 34, 2016)

Left: the IQ modulator. Right: its QPSK constellation, one point per symbol (real part across, imaginary part up); noise spreads each point into a cloud, and the receiver picks the nearest point. A photodiode sees only the squared distance from the origin (the dashed circle: all four QPSK points look the same). A homodyne receiver sees the projection onto its LO axis (dashed amber).

  • Why null, for coherent formats: the field is signed, so symbols sit symmetrically round the origin; no laser power or receiver range is spent on a carrier; and for a small drive the field slope is steepest at null (slide 03).
  • The photonic link module does not do this. Its inputs are unsigned bits on single MZMs at quadrature (slide 06), and the complex values appear only after the optical Fourier transform (slide 10).
06

Why Quadrature Bias Suits Unsigned Bits

At quadrature each lane's field is E0 + d·x, with x a bit (0 or 1): a constant carrier E0 (the '0' level) plus a data step d. The optical Fourier transform is linear, and the DFT of a constant lands only in bin k = 0. derived

DFT([E0, E0, E0, E0] + d·bits) = [4E0, 0, 0, 0] + d·DFT(bits)

Top: the four lane fields (√µW), carrier part (grey) and data part (teal). Bottom: the four outputs Y = DFT(x)/2 (the transform is unitary, so it scales by ½), real parts teal and imaginary parts amber, with the carrier's share hatched. The carrier adds 2E0 = 5.62 √µW to output 1 (130.0 µA of detector current) and nothing to outputs 2–4. Model: chainModel with the illustrative parameters of slide 09.

What follows from the maths derived

  • The carrier only touches output 1, as a fixed offset that calibration removes. Outputs 2–4 see exactly d × DFT(bits) (halved by the unitary transform).
  • Null bias would compute the same thing. Signed inputs ±1 are 2x − 1: again a constant (which lands in output 1) plus a scaled copy of the bits.
  • Null's advantages are real but modest here: a larger field step per volt (slide 03) and no offset to calibrate out.

Why quadrature is still a good choice inference

  • At quadrature '0' and '1' have different powers, so slow monitor photodiodes and intensity-based calibration steps can see the data. At null they cannot.
  • It is the easiest bias to find and hold (slide 07).
  • Datacom drivers and process-design-kit modulators are made and characterised for it.

The patent's word is quadrature (slide 04) patent; the reasons in the right-hand box are this deck's, not the patent's.

Holding the Bias Point

Bias points drift with temperature and ageing, so a slow loop holds them. What the loop can measure depends on where it is holding. textbook

Top left: the transfer curve, the bias (dot) and the dither's excursion either side. Top right: the monitor power over two dither periods (the dither is 0.1 rad, exaggerated in the drawing). Bottom: the three error signals against bias phase, from a numerical lock-in (dither, checked against its Bessel-function form in the tests). The 1st harmonic is drawn ×10 and the 2nd ×200; dots are the recorded values in results.md.

Holding quadrature

  • Power changes to first order with drift. The error is simply average monitor power (normalised to the peak, or ratioed against an input tap) − ½, fed to an integrator: a DC servo. The integrator's sign picks which of the two quadrature points (π/2 or 3π/2, opposite slopes) it holds.

Holding null (or peak)

  • The power is at an extremum: drift either way raises it, so the level alone cannot say which way to move.
  • Add a small dither tone and detect synchronously (lock-in): the 1st-harmonic response is the slope, zero exactly at null, with a sign. The RF equivalent: nulling an IQ mixer's LO leakage.

Direct and Coherent Detection

Direct detection is one photodiode measuring power: enough for intensity data, blind to sign and phase. Coherent detection mixes the signal Es with a local oscillator (LO) of the same frequency in a 2×2 coupler and subtracts the two photodiode currents (a balanced pair): textbook

i = i1 − i2 = 2·R·Re(Es·ELO*) = 2·R·√(Ps·PLO)·cos Δφ

Left: output 2's nine possible fields (√µW, slide 10), the dashed circle being what direct detection can tell apart (only |E|). The LO phase sets the axis (amber); each point's projection on it is what the balanced pair reports. Right: the projected levels as currents (slide 09's scale), and for the highlighted point the two photodiodes' currents: each carries the large common term R(Ps+PLO)/2, which the subtraction removes. Model: balancedPair, chainModel.

What the receiver gets

  • Sign: the output is linear in the field.
  • Gain: the signal grows as √PLO, lifting a weak signal above the amplifier's noise (slide 14).
  • A chosen projection: Δφ = 0 reads the real part, 90° the imaginary part, as in a zero-IF radio. A 90° hybrid gives both at once.
  • Balance cancels the LO's power and most of its intensity noise (to the pair's common-mode rejection).

Kinds of coherent receiver

  • Homodyne: LO at the signal's frequency, phase-locked.
  • Heterodyne: an offset, so an intermediate frequency.
  • Intradyne: nearly the same frequency, phase tracked in DSP (modern telecom).
  • Self-homodyne: the LO split from the same laser, so its phase noise cancels except over any path-length mismatch.

The module: one of the laser's seven channels becomes the LO, through a slow MZI “to control both phase and intensity”, split four ways with a phase shifter each; each copy is mixed with one transform output, and “All coherent homodyne detection techniques used in the PIC use balanced PDs.” paper Self-homodyne, in the terms above. derived

The DFT Alphabet

The transform computes a 4-point DFT, Xk = Σn xn·(−j)kn, whose only weights are 1, −j, −1 and j. With bits for inputs, the outputs take a small, fixed set of values: the module's output alphabet. derived

Step through all 16 inputs (dft4Int). Each panel is one output on the complex plane; points already visited stay. Outputs 1 and 3 fill five levels on the real axis; outputs 2 and 4 fill 3×3 grids that are mirror images (complex conjugates) of each other for every input.

  • The outputs are signed although the inputs are not, so the receivers must be coherent: an intensity detector would lose the sign.
  • Why four detectors suffice. For real inputs X3 = X1* (conjugate symmetry), and X0, X2 are real. Reading Re X0, Re X1, Re X2 and Im X3 recovers all four outputs: three detectors read real parts and one reads an imaginary part, the receiver arrangement a patent cited by the paper describes (WO2025168827A1). patent derived
  • The same patent states the PAM5 (outputs 1 and 3) and 3-level-per-axis (outputs 2 and 4) alphabet patent; the enumeration above checks it.

patent The start-up calibration of WO2025168828A1 (a patent cited by the paper), with the stream paused and known input vectors applied: set the modulators to quadrature; set the system's reference phase with one input switched on; set the LO amplitude to maximum; sweep each LO phase “from 0 to pi radian” until each output matches a DFT lookup table; sweep each input's phase “from 0 to 2pi radian” until all outputs match the truth table; then cycle all 16 vectors with small tweaks.

  • Order matters: each step fixes a reference the next one needs, like calibrating a scope before measuring with it. The animation idealises each step as removing its own errors exactly.
  • The pass criterion is a decision margin: the claimed threshold is “less than a half of the difference between the 0 and 1 binary signal state”, exactly the condition for a correct level decision. patent
  • In service, reference vectors are inserted as marker frames; a frame that fails is re-applied once, and a second failure pauses the stream for recalibration. patent Thermo-optic shifters settle in microseconds (Harris et al., arXiv:1410.3616), textbook, so the loop tracks slow thermal drift. inference

From PAM5 and PAM3 to PAM4

“The transmitted data to the module is restricted to binary (NRZ) levels. This is to compress the photonic computed result to PAM4”, and the results return on the QSFP56 port's receive lanes. paper But outputs 1 and 3 have five levels (slide 10), and PAM4 has four.

Saturation packing, as drawn here: the top level of outputs 1 and 3 is clipped onto the one below it (warning colour). inference This deck's mapping is illustrative; the patents' own level coding differs (for example, raw levels offset per channel so one receiver design serves all four).

What the patent describes patent

  • Two packings that keep the result recoverable (WO2025168827A1): carry add (detect the fifth level on outputs 1 and 3, subtract it, and add two levels to output 2's PAM3) and saturation (clip the fifth level onto the fourth and recover it from the other channels).
  • Saturation “can be done on the receiver itself by reducing the dynamic range of the receiver”: a receiver run into compression does the job.

Why the FPGA can undo the clip inference

  • X0 − X2 = 2(x1 + x3) is even, so outputs 1 and 3 have the same parity: a clipped 3 on output 1 is told from a true 3 by output 3's level.
  • In two of the 16 cases (1110 and 1011 in the table, column “Candidates after parity”) parity leaves two candidates; the impossible one is ruled out because a sum of 4 forces every other output to 0.
  • Carry-add needs logic that sees several channels before the FPGA, which the module does not report; saturation needs none. So saturation looks the likelier route here, and the FPGA's own PAM4 receiver doubles as the module's ADC.

The Receiver: What a TIA Does

“The PDs in the PIC are packaged with Single Layer Capacitors (SLCs) and bonded with a TIA die.” paper A transimpedance amplifier (TIA) turns the balanced pair's difference current into a voltage. The SLCs give the photodiode bias an AC ground right at the diode, and a bare TIA die bonded beside the chip keeps the input capacitance and the bond-wire inductance small. inference

A five-level signal through a linear TIA (levels kept) and a limiting TIA (levels crushed onto its rails). Illustrative transfer curves.

Linear, not limiting textbook

  • A limiting TIA saturates on purpose: fine for NRZ, fatal for computed multi-level results. A linear TIA keeps the levels; its compression shifts the outer ones, a calibration term until it crosses a threshold.
  • Doubling the bandwidth costs four times the gain; doubling the capacitance costs two.

Datacom TIAs and computed levels inference

  • Automatic gain control holds the output swing constant, which suits datacom (the information is in relative levels) but rescales computed ones, and reacts to the data statistics. Fixed (manual) gain, or re-calibrating thresholds when the gain changes, is safer.
  • A balanced pair's current averages near zero. A datacom TIA built for one photodiode's one-way current may read its signal strength as zero, sit at maximum gain, or let its DC-offset loop remove output 1's data-dependent mean.
  • Computed results cannot be scrambled (that would change the answer), so long runs and a high-pass corner cause baseline wander.

The Noise Budget

Is there enough signal-to-noise? Three noise currents compete with the balanced pair's signal: the TIA's own noise (flat), shot noise (grows as √PLO, like the signal) and the LO's intensity noise left after imperfect balance (grows as PLO). A decision fails when noise crosses half a level step, so the figure of merit is Q = (step/2)/σ; Q ≈ 4.9 gives about 10−6 errors per decision. textbook

What to Take Away

The deck in one line.

  • A photodiode sees power, not field. Sign and phase need an interferometer to write them (the MZM) and a reference to read them (the LO).
  • Bias points are a design choice. Quadrature makes power linear and keeps both levels positive; null makes the field linear and signed. For unsigned bits into a DFT, quadrature's carrier costs only an offset on output 1.
  • The DFT of bits has a tiny alphabet: five real levels on two outputs, 3×3 grids on the other two, mirror images. Three real readings and one imaginary recover everything, and saturation packs it into PAM4.
  • Calibration is geometry: output errors rotate the constellation, input errors distort it, and the patent's order fixes the reference, then the receivers, then the transmitters, to within half a level step.
  • The analogue limits are the receiver's: a linear TIA without automatic gain surprises, enough LO to beat the TIA's noise, a noise bandwidth matched to the symbol rate, and lane skew that would matter far more at 56 GBaud.
